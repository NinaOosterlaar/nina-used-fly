## Meiji Shrine

The Meiji Shrine is a prominent Shinto shrine located in Shibuya, Tokyo, dedicated to the deified spirits of Emperor Meiji and his wife, Empress Shōken.
Visitors typically bow at the torii (entrance gates), wash their hands and mouths at the temizuya (water fountain), and practice the traditional bow-bow-clap-bow method at the main hall.
This place is a sense of calm and serenity, with a large forested area surrounding the shrine, providing a peaceful escape from the bustling city.

## Yoyogi Park

Yoyogi Park is one of Tokyo's largest public green spaces, located in Shibuya City right next to Harajuku Station and the Meiji Jingu Shrine.
Spans roughly 54 hectares (134 acres) and is divided into a forested park side (with lawns, walking paths, ponds, and a dog run) and an event plaza side (with an open-air stage and sports courts).
The site has a varied past—it hosted Japan's first powered aircraft flight in 1910, served as a military parade ground and U.S. military housing (Washington Heights), and functioned as the Olympic Village for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics before opening as a public park in 1967.
Often called Tokyo’s "outdoor living room," it draws a diverse crowd of joggers, picnickers, street performers, cosplayers, and weekend subcultures (such as rockabilly dancers).

## Sensō-ji Temple

Senso-ji (also called Asakusa Kannon Temple) is Tokyo's oldest Buddhist temple, originally founded in 628 AD in the Asakusa district.
Dedicated to Kannon Bosatsu (the Bodhisattva of Mercy and Compassion), whose sacred golden statue was reportedly found in the nearby Sumida River by two fishermen in 628.
The origin legend: After the brothers repeatedly tried to return the statue to the river and it miraculously came back to them, the local village chief enshrined it for public worship.

Main Attractions:
- Kaminarimon (Thunder Gate): The iconic outer entrance featuring a massive 700 kg red paper lantern.
- Nakamise-dori: A historic, bustling shopping street leading up to the temple filled with traditional snacks, sweets, and souvenirs.
- Hozomon Gate & Five-Storied Pagoda: Major architectural landmarks within the inner temple grounds.
- Main Hall (Hondo): Where worshippers gather to pray and offer incense (which is traditionally wafted over the body for health and healing).

## Edo-Tokyo Museum

The Edo-Tokyo Museum is a large history museum in Tokyo, Japan, that showcases the city's 400-year transformation from the samurai-era Edo period into a modern metropolis.

Visitors can walk across a full-scale model of the Nihonbashi (a famous historic bridge) and see a replica of the traditional Nakamura-za theater.
The museum displays detailed miniatures of old neighborhoods, buildings, and merchant streets from different eras.

## Imperial Palace 

The Tokyo Imperial Palace (Kōkyo) is the official primary residence of the Emperor of Japan.
Kyūden) for state ceremonies, the Fukiage Palace (the Emperor's living quarters), private residences for the Imperial Family, archives, and administrative offices.
The actual residential and interior buildings are not open to the general public for free-roaming. Public entry to the inner grounds is limited to two special days a year: January 2 (New Year's Greeting) and February 23 (Emperor's Birthday), when the Imperial Family appears on the balcony.

There are two areas that are open to the public.
The first is the East Gardens. Imperial Palace East National Gardens are a free public green space in central Tokyo built on the historical ruins of Edo Castle.
Key features of the East Gardens include the remains of Edo Castle. Massive stone walls, deep moats, the foundation of the former castle keep, and historic guardhouses.
Also the Ninomaru Garden, a traditional Japanese landscape featuring a koi pond, waterfall, and seasonal flowers.
There is also a museum.

## Hie Shrine

Hie Shrine is a historic Shinto shrine located in the heart of Tokyo.
Dedicated to Oyamakui-no-kami, a traditional Shinto god of mountains and water.
Founded in 1478 by the samurai warlord Ōta Dōkan, it historically served as the guardian shrine (ujigami) of Edo Castle and the Tokugawa shogunate.
One of the main features is a scenic back-entrance path featuring dozens of bright red torii gates (similar to Kyoto's Fushimi Inari).
Additionally there are monkey statues (masaru) that replace the usual guardian dogs (komainu), seen as divine messengers bringing good fortune.

## Ginza Area

Next up, more shopping.
Ginza is Tokyo’s premier upmarket shopping, dining, and entertainment district, located in the Chūō Ward of Tokyo, Japan.
The name comes from gin (silver) and za (guild or mint), as the Japanese government moved its silver coin mint to this location in 1612 during the Edo period.
Following a massive fire in 1872 and the 1923 Kantō earthquake, the area was rebuilt with Western-style brick buildings and became a symbol of modern civilization and the birthplace of window-shopping (gin-bura) in Japan.
On weekend afternoons, the main thoroughfare (Chūō-dōri) is closed to vehicular traffic, turning the wide street into a pedestrian-only zone for walking and outdoor seating.
Home to historic Japanese department stores like Ginza Mitsukoshi, Matsuya Ginza, and Wako (famous for its Hattori clocktower), alongside modern mega-complexes like GINZA SIX and flagship boutiques for international luxury brands.
It also features the historic Kabukiza Theatre (the main stage for traditional kabuki performances) and over 100 independent art and design galleries.
Renowned for high-end Edomae-style sushi masters, Michelin-starred establishments, historic cafes (like Kissa You), and deep-pocketed cocktail bars tucked into backstreets and beneath railway tracks.

## Owakudani

Then we take the ropeway to Owakudani, which is a volcanic valley with active sulfur vents and hot springs in Hakone, Kanagawa Prefecture, Japan.

The area is famous for its black eggs (kuro-tamago), which are boiled in the naturally hot sulfuric waters. Eating one is said to add seven years to your life.
Owakudani is also a popular tourist destination for its scenic views of Mount Fuji, hiking trails, and ropeway rides. The area has a unique, otherworldly landscape due to the volcanic activity.

## Lake Ashi

We then take the ropeway to Togendai and go on to Lake Ashi.

## Ueno Park

Ueno Park is a large, public cultural park located in the Taito district of Tokyo, Japan.
Established in 1873, it is one of Japan's oldest official public parks.
The grounds originally belonged to the influential Kan'ei-ji Temple, much of which was destroyed during the Battle of Ueno in 1868.
It spans over 530,000 square meters and draws more than ten million visitors every year.
It has a zoo, which is Japan's oldest zoo opened in 1882.
There are also multiple museums.
Additionally there is a nice large pond and a shrine, the Ueno Tōshō-gū, dedicated to Tokugawa Ieyasu, the founder of the Tokugawa shogunate.

## Yanesan Area

Yanesen is a nostalgic shitamachi (old downtown) district in Tokyo covering the neighborhoods of Yanaka, Nezu, and Sendagi.
It is a retro neighborhood with narrow alleys, wooden houses, and a calm pace that survived major wartime destruction.
Yanaka Ginza is a shopping street with traditional shops, cafés, and local crafts.
Nezu Shrine is a historic Shinto shrine famous for its vibrant torii gates and azalea gardens.
But my main goal is to go to Yanaka Cemetery.

### Yanaka Cemetery

Yanaka Cemetery is a large, historic public cemetery located in the traditional Yanaka neighborhood of Taito Ward, Tokyo.
Established in 1872 during the Meiji period, the land originally belonged to the Buddhist temple Tennō-ji. It was secularized and turned into a public burial ground as part of government policies separating Buddhism and Shintoism.
Spans roughly 10 hectares (over 100,000 square meters) and holds approximately 7,000 graves. It is so large that it even has its own small police station (koban) inside the grounds.
Unlike many Western cemeteries, Yanaka has a relaxed, park-like feel where locals walk, cycle, and enjoy the quiet paths alongside neighborhood cats and old temples.
It has a really Japanese feel.

## Odaiba

Afterwards, we have to travel a bit, but we will go to Odaiba, which is a large artificial island in Tokyo Bay, Japan.
Built in 1853 by the Tokugawa shogunate as a series of defensive fort islands (daiba meaning artillery battery platforms) to protect Tokyo from marine attacks.
Expanded and redeveloped in the 1990s into a futuristic commercial and residential zone.
Some key attractions:
- Rainbow Bridge: An iconic two-tiered suspension bridge connecting the island to central Tokyo, featuring beautiful nighttime illuminations.
- Large complexes like AQUA CiTY Odaiba and DECKS Tokyo Beach, which house indoor theme parks and food alleys.
- A replica of the Statue of Liberty and popular pop-culture displays.
- The Miraikan (National Museum of Emerging Science and Innovation).
